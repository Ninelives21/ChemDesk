8.1 Ionic Bond / Electrovalent Bond

Approx. video segment: 00:45–05:48

An ionic bond is also called an electrovalent bond. It forms when a metal completely transfers electron(s) to a non-metal.

  • The metal loses electron(s) and becomes a positive ion or cation.
  • The non-metal gains electron(s) and becomes a negative ion or anion.
  • The main aim is to get a stable electronic configuration, usually an octet.

Example: Formation of NaCl

Sodium has atomic number 11.

Na: 2,8,1 → Na⁺: 2,8 + e⁻

Chlorine has electronic configuration 2,8,7.

Cl: 2,8,7 + e⁻ → Cl⁻: 2,8,8

Sodium loses one electron and chlorine gains that electron. So Na⁺ and Cl⁻ are formed.

Important point: The electron loss and electron gain are considered for gaseous atoms/ions first. After that, the oppositely charged ions come together and form solid NaCl.

Why do Na⁺ and Cl⁻ stay together?

Na⁺ and Cl⁻ have opposite charges, so they attract each other strongly. This attraction is called electrostatic force of attraction.

F = Kq₁q₂ / r²

  • q₁ and q₂ are the charges.
  • r is the distance between the ions.
  • When ions come close, r becomes small, so attraction becomes strong.

This strong attraction holds the ions in a solid lattice. That is why ionic compounds are usually strong and solid.

Do not draw a dash bond: Ionic compounds are not shown with a single line like covalent compounds. There is no fixed directional bond between two atoms. The attraction is non-directional.

  • Ionic bonds have no fixed shape.
  • Ionic bonds are non-directional.
  • A negative ion can attract a positive ion from any direction.

8.2 Lewis Dot Structure in Ionic Bonds

Approx. video segment: 06:02–09:00

Lewis dot structure shows the valence-shell electrons. For ionic compounds, show which electron is lost by the metal and gained by the non-metal.

NaCl Lewis dot structure

  • Na has one valence electron.
  • Cl has seven valence electrons.
  • Na gives its one electron to Cl.
  • Na becomes Na⁺ and Cl becomes Cl⁻.
  • Cl⁻ now has eight electrons in its outer shell.

One electron on chlorine may be shown differently, such as by a cross, to show that it came from sodium.

MgCl₂ Lewis dot structure

  • Magnesium has atomic number 12: 2,8,2.
  • Mg loses two electrons and becomes Mg²⁺.
  • Two chlorine atoms are needed because each chlorine accepts one electron.
  • Each chlorine becomes Cl⁻ with a complete octet.

Mg → Mg²⁺ + 2e⁻

2Cl + 2e⁻ → 2Cl⁻

Final ionic arrangement:

Mg²⁺ + 2Cl⁻ → MgCl₂

Two acceptable ways to show LDS:

  • Show the electrons before transfer: metal electron(s) moving towards non-metal.
  • Show the ions after transfer: cation with positive charge and anion with complete octet.

8.3 Electrovalency

Approx. video segment: 09:00–12:46

Electrovalency means the number of electrons lost by a metal or gained by a non-metal during formation of an electrovalent bond.

Remember: Electrovalency is written as a positive number because it counts electrons lost or gained. If chlorine gains one electron, its electrovalency is 1, not -1.

Group-wise electrovalency

Group Examples Usual electrovalency Reason
Group 1 Li, Na, K, Rb, Cs 1 They lose 1 valence electron.
Group 2 Be, Mg, Ca, Sr, Ba 2 They lose 2 valence electrons.
Group 13 Al, Ga, etc. 3 They can lose 3 valence electrons.
Group 14 C, Si, Ge, etc. Not usually used for ionic bond formation They have 4 valence electrons, so they usually prefer sharing instead of losing or gaining 4 electrons.
Group 15 N, P, As, etc. 3 They gain 3 electrons to complete octet.
Group 16 O, S, Se, etc. 2 They gain 2 electrons to complete octet.
Group 17 F, Cl, Br, I 1 They gain 1 electron to complete octet.
Group 3 to 12 d-block / transition elements Variable Their valency can change from compound to compound.

Variable electrovalency examples

  • Fe can show 2 and 3.
  • Cu can show 1 and 2.

Review-only check: Use Group 3 to 12 for d-block elements. If a source line says “Group 2 to 12” for d-block, keep it for review and do not put it in final public notes without correction.

8.4 Energy Terms Involved in Ionic Bond Formation / Favourable Conditions

Approx. video segment: 12:58–18:14

Ionic bond formation is favourable when the final compound becomes more stable. Stability is connected with energy.

  • If energy is released, stability increases.
  • If energy has to be absorbed, the absorbed energy should be as small as possible.

Step 1: Ionization Energy

A metal atom loses electron(s) in gaseous state to form a cation. Energy is required for this step.

M(g) → M⁺(g) + e⁻

  • This required energy is called ionization energy.
  • For easy ionic bond formation, ionization energy should be low.

IE → minimum

Step 2: Electron Affinity

A non-metal atom gains electron(s) in gaseous state to form an anion. Energy is usually released in this step.

X(g) + e⁻ → X⁻(g)

  • This energy released is related to electron affinity.
  • For stable ionic compound formation, electron affinity should be high.

EA → maximum

Step 3: Lattice Energy

The gaseous cation and gaseous anion come together to form a solid ionic compound. Energy is released here also.

M⁺(g) + X⁻(g) → MX(s)

Lattice energy is the energy released when one mole of an ionic compound is formed from its gaseous cations and gaseous anions.

  • More lattice energy released means a more stable ionic solid.
  • It also means more energy will be needed to break the lattice again.

LE → maximum

Favourable conditions for ionic bond formation

Condition Should be Why?
Ionization energy of metal Minimum Metal should lose electron easily.
Electron affinity of non-metal Maximum Non-metal should gain electron easily and release energy.
Lattice energy Maximum Strong solid lattice should form with high stability.

8.5 Which Is a Stronger Ionic Bond?

Approx. video segment: 18:17–20:39

Ionic character is more when the metal is strongly metallic and the non-metal is strongly non-metallic.

  • Metal should lose electron easily.
  • Non-metal should gain electron strongly.
  • Greater difference in metallic and non-metallic character usually means more ionic character.

Francium is taken as a highly metallic element because it is at the bottom of Group 1. Fluorine is highly non-metallic. So FrF is a strong ionic example in this basic comparison style.

Example 1: NaF, NaCl, NaBr, NaI

The metal is the same in all: Na. So compare the non-metal.

  • Non-metallic character: F > Cl > Br > I
  • Most ionic: NaF
  • Most covalent character among these: NaI

Example 2: LiCl, NaCl, KCl, RbCl

The non-metal is the same in all: Cl. So compare the metal.

  • Metallic character increases down Group 1.
  • Most ionic: RbCl
  • Most covalent character among these: LiCl

Quick method: If one side is fixed, compare the other side using periodic trends. More metallic metal + more non-metallic non-metal gives more ionic character.

8.6 Properties of Ionic Compounds

Main segment: 20:47–33:13

8.6.1 Physical State

Approx. video segment: 20:47–21:58

  • Ionic compounds are usually solids.
  • They form a 3D lattice.
  • The ions are held together by strong electrostatic forces.

NaCl is commonly discussed as a rock-salt type structure. A full crystal has a repeated 3D arrangement of ions.

8.6.2 Melting Point and Boiling Point

Approx. video segment: 21:58–22:31

  • Ionic compounds have very high melting points.
  • Ionic compounds have very high boiling points.

Reason: the ions are strongly attracted to each other. A lot of energy is needed to separate the ions.

8.6.3 Conductivity

Approx. video segment: 22:32–25:42

Electricity is carried when charges can move. Ionic compounds contain ions, but the ions are not always free to move.

State Conductivity Reason
Solid state Bad conductor Ions are fixed in the lattice and cannot move freely.
Molten state Good conductor Heat separates the ions enough for movement.
Aqueous state Good conductor Water reduces attraction between ions, so ions become free to move.

Water has a strong dielectric property. It reduces the force of attraction between opposite charges by about 81 times.

Common exam trap: Do not write “ionic compounds always conduct electricity.” Solid ionic compounds do not conduct well. Molten and aqueous ionic compounds conduct because ions are mobile.

8.6.4 Isomerism

Approx. video segment: 25:42–27:30

Isomerism means same molecular formula but different structural formula.

  • Isomerism is common in covalent compounds, especially organic compounds.
  • Ionic compounds do not have fixed directional bonds like covalent compounds.
  • So ionic compounds do not show normal structural isomerism.

A single NaCl unit is not treated like a fixed molecule with a fixed shape. Ionic compounds are better understood as lattices of ions.

8.6.5 Isomorphism

Approx. video segment: 27:30–30:23

Isomorphism means two ionic compounds have identical or very similar crystalline / lattice / 3D structures.

Isomorphism is expected when:

  1. The formula pattern is same or similar.
  2. The sizes of the cations and anions are nearly the same.

Examples used for this idea

  • NaF and MgO: Na and Mg are neighbouring elements; F and O are also neighbouring elements, so their ion sizes can be close.
  • NaCl and KCl: not a good isomorphism example here because Na⁺ and K⁺ have quite different sizes.

Review-only check before final publishing: The NaCl / MgCl₂ or MgCl comparison needs source and Chemistry review before it is used as a final student-facing example. Keep the rule of isomorphism, but verify this example carefully.

8.6.6 Type of Reaction

Approx. video segment: 30:23–32:42

Ionic compounds usually give ionic reactions. These reactions are very fast because ions are already present and can combine quickly.

  • Ionic reactions are much faster than many covalent / molecular reactions.
  • Precipitation reactions are especially fast.
  • Approximate time mentioned for such fast ionic reactions: 10⁻⁶ s.

Example: Precipitation of AgCl

NaCl(aq) + AgNO₃(aq) → AgCl(s) + NaNO₃(aq)

In aqueous solution, ions are free. Ag⁺ and Cl⁻ quickly combine to form a white precipitate of AgCl.

MCQ clue: If options contain one ionic precipitation reaction and other slow molecular/organic reactions, the ionic precipitation reaction is usually the fastest.

8.6.7 Solubility — Hydration Energy / Solvation Energy

Approx. video segment: 32:42–33:13

Ionic compounds are generally more soluble in polar solvents like water than covalent compounds.

  • Water surrounds the ions and stabilizes them.
  • This process is called hydration in water.
  • In general solvents, the same idea is called solvation.
  • Solubility depends on the balance between lattice energy and hydration / solvation energy.

The detailed comparison of lattice energy and solvation energy can be studied separately.

Final quick revision:

  • Ionic bond = complete electron transfer + electrostatic attraction.
  • Do not draw a dash bond for ionic compounds.
  • Electrovalency is always counted as positive.
  • Favourable conditions: IE minimum, EA maximum, LE maximum.
  • More metallic metal + more non-metallic non-metal = more ionic character.
  • Ionic compounds are solid, high MP/BP, conduct in molten/aqueous state, do not show normal isomerism, can show isomorphism, and usually give fast ionic reactions.
