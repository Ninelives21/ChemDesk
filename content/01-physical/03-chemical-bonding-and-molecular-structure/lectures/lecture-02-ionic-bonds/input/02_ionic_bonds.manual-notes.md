## 1. Ionic Bond / Electrovalent Bond

**Video segment:** https://www.youtube.com/embed/OqdNZTHxPxM?start=45&end=179

- Ionic bonds form between a metal and a non-metal.
- There is complete transfer of electron(s), so the octet is completed.
- Ionic bonds are non-directional.
- Do not draw a “dash” between atoms in an ionic bond.
- Example:

  \[
  \text{Na} \rightarrow \text{Na}^+ + \text{e}^-
  \]

  \[
  \text{Cl} + \text{e}^- \rightarrow \text{Cl}^-
  \]

- Note: this transfer of electron is happening in the gaseous states of Na and Cl.
- \(\text{Na}^+\) and \(\text{Cl}^-\) attract each other through a strong electrostatic force of attraction.
- Electrostatic force:

  \[
  F = \dfrac{K q_1 q_2}{r^2}
  \]

  Note that \(r\) is very small.

- Ionic bonds do not have a shape or direction.

## 3. Electrovalency

**Video segment:** https://www.youtube.com/embed/OqdNZTHxPxM?start=540&end=766

- Electrovalency is the number of electrons given by the metal or the number of electrons gained by the non-metal during formation of an electrovalent bond.
- Electrovalency is always taken as positive.
- Electrovalency of elements group-wise:

  1. Group 1 elements — Li, Na, K, etc. \(\rightarrow\) 1
  2. Group 2 elements — Be, Mg, Ca, Sr, etc. \(\rightarrow\) 2
  3. Group 13 elements — B, Al, Ga, etc. \(\rightarrow\) 3
  4. Group 14 elements — C, Si, Ge, etc. \(\rightarrow\) do not form ionic bonds
  5. Group 15 elements — N, P, As, etc. \(\rightarrow\) 3
  6. Group 16 elements — O, S, Se, etc. \(\rightarrow\) 2
  7. Group 17 elements — F, Cl, Br, etc. \(\rightarrow\) 1
  8. Group 3 to 12 elements, the d-block elements, show variable valencies.
     - Example: Fe \(\rightarrow\) 2, 3
     - Example: Cu \(\rightarrow\) 1, 2

## 4. Energy Terms Involved in Ionic Bond Formation / Favourable Conditions

**Video segment:** https://www.youtube.com/embed/OqdNZTHxPxM?start=778&end=1094

- When energy is released, a stable compound is formed.
- If energy must be absorbed, the amount absorbed should be as low as possible.

The three kinds of energies involved in formation of ionic compounds are:

### 4.1 Ionization Energy

- Energy absorbed to remove an electron from a metal in its gaseous state.
- For a stable compound, ionization energy should be as low as possible.

  \[
  \text{IE} \rightarrow \min
  \]

### 4.2 Electron Affinity

- Energy released when a non-metal accepts an electron.
- For a stable compound, electron affinity should be as high as possible.

  \[
  \text{EA} \rightarrow \max
  \]

### 4.3 Lattice Energy

- Energy released when a gaseous cation and a gaseous anion combine to form one mole of a solid ionic compound.
- Conversely, lattice energy can be thought of as the energy needed to break an ionic compound.
- For a stable compound, lattice energy should be as high as possible.

  \[
  \text{LE} \rightarrow \max
  \]

## 5. Which Is a Stronger Ionic Bond?

**Video segment:** https://www.youtube.com/embed/OqdNZTHxPxM?start=1097&end=1239

- The metal should have more metallic character.
- Example: Francium is considered the most metallic element because it is at the bottom of Group 1. This gives it a large atomic radius, weak effective nuclear attraction for the valence electron, and high metallic character.
- The non-metal should have more non-metallic character.
- Example of a strong ionic bond: FrF.

### Example comparisons

- Which has more ionic character among NaF, NaCl, NaBr, NaI?

  \[
  \text{NaF}
  \]

- Which has more covalent character among NaF, NaCl, NaBr, NaI?

  \[
  \text{NaI}
  \]

- Which has more ionic character among LiCl, NaCl, KCl, RbCl?

  \[
  \text{RbCl}
  \]

## 6. Properties of Ionic Compounds

**Main video segment:** https://www.youtube.com/embed/OqdNZTHxPxM?start=1247&end=2005

### 6.1 Physical State

**Video segment:** https://www.youtube.com/embed/OqdNZTHxPxM?start=1259&end=1328

- Ionic compounds are solids.
- They form a solid lattice due to strong electrostatic forces.

### 6.2 Melting Point and Boiling Point

**Video segment:** https://www.youtube.com/embed/OqdNZTHxPxM?start=1328&end=1353

- Ionic compounds have very high melting and boiling points.
- Reason: strong electrostatic forces.

### 6.3 Conductivity

**Video segment:** https://www.youtube.com/embed/OqdNZTHxPxM?start=1353&end=1550

- Ionic compounds are bad conductors in the solid state because the ions are held together by strong electrostatic forces and are not free to move.
- Ionic compounds are good conductors in molten state and aqueous state.
- In molten state, heat energy allows the ions to move and conduct electricity.
- In aqueous state, water reduces the force of attraction between ions by a factor of 81 due to its dielectric property.

### 6.4 Isomerism

**Video segment:** https://www.youtube.com/embed/OqdNZTHxPxM?start=1550&end=1662

- Isomerism means same molecular formula but different structural formula.
- Ionic compounds do not display isomerism because they have no fixed molecular structure.

### 6.5 Isomorphism

**Video segment:** https://www.youtube.com/embed/OqdNZTHxPxM?start=1662&end=1840

- Isomorphism is the property where two or more ionic compounds share identical or very similar crystalline, lattice, or 3D structures.

Isomorphism takes place when:

1. Ions have similar formula.
2. The size of ions is nearly the same.
   - Example: Na and Mg
   - Example: F and O
3. Example: NaCl and \(\text{MgCl}_2\)
4. Example: NaF and MgO
5. Example of compounds not displaying isomorphism: NaCl and KCl, because Na and K are very different in size.

### 6.6 Type of Reaction

**Video segment:** https://www.youtube.com/embed/OqdNZTHxPxM?start=1840&end=1962

- Ionic reactions are very fast, especially precipitation reactions.
- Approximate time to complete: \(10^{-6}\) seconds.
- It is difficult to measure the speed of ionic reactions.

### 6.7 Solubility — Hydration Energy / Solvation Energy

**Video segment:** https://www.youtube.com/embed/OqdNZTHxPxM?start=1962&end=2005

- Ionic compounds are generally more soluble in polar solvents than covalent compounds.
- Polar solvents like water stabilize separated ions, making it more favourable for ionic compounds to dissolve.
